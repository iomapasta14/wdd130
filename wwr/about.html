Right It! - The WWR Company

Our purpose is to put people in a situation where they discover they are more capable than they believed, and to make sure they get to feel that on the water rather than just hear about it in a briefing.

The river decides if you flip. You decide if you finish.

History

It started with a boat going wrong. Three of them had no reason to be on the same river that afternoon. One had driven five hours to clear his head after quitting a job he had been good at and miserable in. One was guiding her fourteenth season and had started to suspect she was only repeating the same day with different faces. The third had never been in a raft in his life and had booked the trip on a dare from a friend who did not show up.

They ended up in the same boat because of a cancelation, which is how most important things begin.

The line was read wrong. The hole caught them at an angle nobody called out in time, and the raft went over with the strange, unhurried violence that water has when it decides something. Underneath, the world got loud and brown and cold, and everyone did the thing they had been taught to do in a briefing nobody fully remembers until the moment it matters.

Then the shout came from the stern, clear and almost cheerful: right it.

Two of them pushed on the same side. The third grabbed the flip line without being told. The boat came back the way it had gone over, heavy and clumsy, and they dragged themselves in with the sort of laughing that comes after fear has let go of you. Nobody had broken anything. Nobody had lost anything.

Somewhere downstream, it stopped being an anecdote and became an idea. The three of them had each boarded that boat needing to be fixed in some way, and the river had refused to do it for them. It only handed them a situation and a single instruction. Right it. That was the whole offer. The rest had to come from the crew.

They spent the following two years doing the unglamorous work. Permits, insurance, a secondhand trailer, a fleet of rafts that leaked at the seams, a website built badly and rebuilt well. The first season was twelve guests. The second was two hundred and forty. Somewhere in there they stopped talking about a rafting company and started talking about the only thing they actually knew how to teach, which was that a crew that gets flipped does not have to be a crew that gets rescued.

The name stuck because the instruction did. Nine seasons later, every guide on the river still ends the safety briefing the same way, and every crew still hears it from the stern before the boat pushes off. It is not a promise that nothing goes wrong. It is a promise about what happens next.
